World Edges Past 1.5C Threshold, Focus Shifts to Net-Negative Emissions

Recent scientific assessments indicate that global warming is almost certain to surpass the 1.5°C limit established by the Paris Agreement in the coming years. This development is intensifying the focus on 'net-negative' emissions, a state where more carbon dioxide is removed from the atmosphere than emitted. While aggressive emissions reductions remain paramount, scientists and policymakers are increasingly looking towards Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) technologies as a crucial strategy to potentially reverse warming trends or stabilize temperatures after an 'overshoot'. The article explores the scientific context, technological challenges, policy implications, and the global imperative to manage this new climate reality.

### World Edges Past 1.5C Threshold, Focus Shifts to Net-Negative Emissions **Introduction** The scientific community has largely converged on a sobering reality: the world is now almost certain to warm past 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, a critical threshold established by the 2015 Paris Agreement. This landmark environmental objective, once seen as a challenging but achievable goal, is increasingly viewed as an impending breach, signalling a profound shift in global climate strategy. Yet, amidst these projections, the discourse is evolving to embrace the concept of 'net-negative' emissions as a necessary, albeit complex, pathway forward. This new phase in climate action acknowledges that while drastic emissions reductions remain non-negotiable, deliberate removal of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere will likely be essential. The imperative is not merely to balance emissions, but to actively extract historical and residual greenhouse gases, aiming to reverse warming or stabilize temperatures after a period of overshoot. The global response now faces the dual challenge of accelerating decarbonization while simultaneously developing and deploying large-scale carbon removal technologies. **Background** The 1.5°C limit originated from the Paris Agreement, a legally binding international treaty on climate change adopted by 196 Parties in December 2015. Its central aim is to strengthen the global response to the threat of climate change by holding the increase in the global average temperature to 'well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels' and pursuing efforts 'to limit the temperature increase to 1.5°C'. Scientists underscored 1.5°C as a safer boundary to avoid the most catastrophic impacts of climate change, emphasizing its significance for vulnerable ecosystems and communities. Pre-industrial levels typically refer to the period between 1850 and 1900. Since then, human activities, primarily the burning of fossil fuels, have already led to a global average temperature rise of approximately 1.1°C to 1.2°C. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the leading international body for assessing climate change, has consistently highlighted the accelerating pace of warming and the narrowing window for effective action. **Timeline** The journey toward understanding and addressing climate change has been marked by critical milestones. In 1992, the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) was established, laying the groundwork for international climate cooperation. This was followed by the Kyoto Protocol in 1997, which set the first legally binding emissions reduction targets for developed countries. The IPCC's Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) in 2013 brought increased attention to carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies. A pivotal moment arrived with the 2015 Paris Agreement, which firmly established the 1.5°C target. In 2018, the IPCC Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5°C further clarified the severe consequences of exceeding this limit and detailed pathways, many of which explicitly included the deployment of carbon dioxide removal (CDR) technologies. The most recent IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), published between 2021 and 2023, has reinforced the urgency. Its findings indicate that exceeding 1.5°C is highly probable in the near term, even with ambitious emissions cuts, making CDR an increasingly integral component of future climate strategies. This period has also seen a significant uptick in private sector investment and government initiatives aimed at developing and scaling various CDR approaches. **Latest Developments** Recent analyses from organizations such as the Copernicus Climate Change Service and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) confirm that global temperature records are being broken with unprecedented frequency. Several scientific reports released in the past year have projected with near certainty that the 1.5°C threshold will be surpassed, at least temporarily, within the next decade. This underscores a collective failure to reduce greenhouse gas emissions quickly enough. In response, the conversation around Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) technologies has intensified. Pilot projects for Direct Air Capture (DAC) facilities, which chemically filter CO2 directly from the atmosphere, are emerging in various locations, albeit at a small scale. Research into enhanced rock weathering, bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), and advanced afforestation techniques is also accelerating, driven by both public and private funding. Policy discussions globally are now frequently incorporating CDR into national climate strategies, moving beyond solely focusing on emissions reduction. **Analysis** The implications of exceeding 1.5°C are profound and far-reaching. Scientists warn of an increase in the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, including more severe heatwaves, prolonged droughts, heavier rainfall, and further acceleration of sea-level rise. Ecological systems, particularly coral reefs and polar regions, face heightened risks of irreversible damage. The term 'overshoot' describes a scenario where global temperatures temporarily exceed 1.5°C before being brought back down, theoretically, through large-scale CDR. Achieving net-negative emissions involves deploying a portfolio of CDR technologies at a monumental scale – potentially billions of tonnes of CO2 per year. Direct Air Capture, while promising, is currently energy-intensive and expensive. Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS) could provide emissions-negative energy, but requires vast land areas for biomass cultivation, raising concerns about food security and biodiversity. Nature-based solutions like afforestation and reforestation are cost-effective but limited by land availability and permanence issues (e.g., wildfires). The integration of CDR with deep decarbonization efforts is critical. CDR is not a substitute for aggressively cutting emissions, but rather a complementary strategy. Experts caution against the 'moral hazard' argument, where the promise of future carbon removal might reduce the urgency to reduce current emissions. Instead, a dual approach is emphasized, requiring simultaneous, ambitious efforts on both fronts. **Public Response** Public awareness of the climate crisis has grown significantly, fueled by more frequent extreme weather events and increased media coverage. Environmental activist groups, such as Fridays for Future and Extinction Rebellion, continue to advocate for urgent, transformative action, often expressing skepticism about the reliance on unproven or energy-intensive technological solutions as a primary fix. Debates around climate justice are also prominent. Concerns are raised about who bears the costs and potential local impacts of large-scale CDR deployment, particularly in developing nations. There is a delicate balance to strike between the perceived necessity of these technologies and public trust, especially given the potential for 'greenwashing' by industries seeking to offset continued high emissions rather than reduce them at source. **Industry Impact** The prospect of net-negative emissions is reshaping various industries. Energy companies, particularly those in the fossil fuel sector, are increasingly exploring and investing in carbon capture and storage as a means to decarbonize their operations and extend their relevance. This includes significant investments in research and development for DAC and other capture technologies. A nascent but rapidly growing carbon removal industry is emerging, with startups and technology firms vying to develop scalable, cost-effective solutions. Companies like Microsoft and Stripe have committed to purchasing carbon removal credits, creating early demand and signaling market interest. However, scaling these technologies from pilot projects to industrial capacities requires substantial capital investment, technological breakthroughs, and supportive policy frameworks. Traditional industries, such as agriculture and forestry, also have a critical role through nature-based CDR solutions. The development of robust carbon markets and verifiable accounting standards is crucial for driving investment and ensuring the integrity of carbon removal projects across all sectors. **Future Outlook** The future outlook for managing global warming increasingly hinges on a two-pronged strategy: aggressive and immediate decarbonization coupled with the rapid, responsible scaling of carbon dioxide removal. Scenarios outlined by the IPCC suggest that achieving net-negative emissions will require an unprecedented global mobilization of resources and innovation, demanding gigatonne-scale deployment of CDR by mid-century. Policy frameworks will need to evolve rapidly to provide clear incentives, regulatory certainty, and robust monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) for CDR projects. International cooperation, technology transfer, and equitable burden-sharing will be essential to ensure that carbon removal solutions are deployed effectively and fairly across the globe. Continued investment in research and development is vital to improve the efficiency, reduce the cost, and mitigate the environmental impacts of these technologies. The long-term vision aims not just to stabilize climate change, but potentially to draw down atmospheric CO2 concentrations to levels that could allow global temperatures to return to safer thresholds. This ambitious undertaking will define climate action for decades to come, reflecting humanity's determined effort to navigate a complex, warming world. **Conclusion** The scientific certainty of exceeding the 1.5°C warming threshold presents humanity with an urgent and multi-faceted challenge. While the primary imperative remains the drastic reduction of greenhouse gas emissions, the conversation has irrevocably shifted to include the necessity of net-negative emissions. Carbon Dioxide Removal technologies, once considered speculative, are now seen as a critical, albeit still developing, component of any viable long-term climate strategy. Navigating this new climate reality demands sustained innovation, significant investment, robust international cooperation, and careful consideration of ethical and social implications. The path to a stable climate future will require not only preventing new emissions but also actively healing the atmosphere, an endeavor that will test human ingenuity and collective resolve like never before.
