WWII POW Ben Skardon, Bataan Death March Survivor, Dies at 101

Colonel Ben Hagood Skardon, a revered World War II Prisoner of War and an icon of resilience, passed away at the age of 101, eliciting a widespread outpouring of tributes. Skardon, who endured the horrific Bataan Death March and years in Japanese captivity, later became a beloved English professor and an enduring symbol of courage through his annual participation in the Bataan Memorial Death March. His death marks the passing of an extraordinary individual whose life illuminated the indomitable human spirit and the profound sacrifices of the Greatest Generation.

CLEMSON, SC — Colonel Ben Hagood Skardon, a celebrated World War II Prisoner of War (POW) and an inspirational figure known for his resilience and unwavering spirit, died on March 16, 2020, at his home in Clemson, South Carolina. He was 101 years old. Skardon's passing has prompted a significant outpouring of support and tributes from across the nation, honoring a life marked by unimaginable hardship and extraordinary dedication to remembrance. His journey from the brutal jungles of Bataan to the classrooms of Clemson University and the deserts of New Mexico forged a legacy that transcended military service, touching countless lives. Born on July 19, 1918, in Pickens, South Carolina, Skardon's early life was rooted in the Southern tradition. He graduated from Clemson A&M College in 1938, where he was commissioned as a Second Lieutenant in the U.S. Army. His military career quickly took him to the Philippines, a key U.S. outpost in the Pacific. As commander of Company A, 1st Battalion, 31st Infantry Regiment, Philippine Division, Skardon found himself on the front lines when Japan launched its invasion of the Philippines in December 1941. He bravely fought during the Battle of Bataan, a brutal campaign that saw American and Filipino forces make a valiant but ultimately futile stand against overwhelming odds. The fall of Bataan on April 9, 1942, marked the beginning of Skardon’s harrowing three-and-a-half years as a prisoner of war. He was among the tens of thousands of American and Filipino soldiers forced to endure the infamous Bataan Death March, a 65-mile trek under brutal conditions, without food or water, leading to the deaths of thousands. Skardon’s captivity continued in various Japanese POW camps, including Camp O'Donnell and Cabanatuan. He battled severe malnutrition, malaria, beriberi, and dysentery, his weight plummeting to a mere 85 pounds. His survival, by his own account, was due to the selfless care of fellow soldiers who shared their meager rations and provided medical attention, as well as his own incredible will to live. He was finally liberated in August 1945 from a prison camp in Mukden, Manchuria, a skeletal shadow of his former self, but with his spirit unbroken. His return home marked the start of a new chapter dedicated to healing and education. Upon his recovery, Skardon returned to his alma mater, Clemson University, where he joined the faculty. He taught English for three decades, from 1948 until his retirement in 1977, shaping generations of students with his intellect and quiet strength. His post-military legacy was perhaps most profoundly defined by his annual participation in the Bataan Memorial Death March at White Sands Missile Range, New Mexico. Colonel Skardon’s death at 101 years old brings to a close an extraordinary chapter in American history. He passed away peacefully at his home, surrounded by loved ones, concluding a life that was both a testament to human endurance and a beacon of hope. His final years were characterized by continued public engagement, drawing strength from the admiration he received. Skardon's life serves as a profound analysis of human resilience and the enduring impact of war. His ability to not only survive but thrive after such trauma, carrying no bitterness, offered a powerful lesson in forgiveness and moving forward. He embodied the spirit of the 'Greatest Generation,' demonstrating courage, sacrifice, and an unwavering commitment to duty and country. His annual presence at the Bataan Memorial Death March transformed the event into more than just a physical challenge; it became a pilgrimage of remembrance and a direct connection to history. His gentle demeanor and powerful story inspired thousands of participants, who considered it an honor to walk in his footsteps. The outpouring of public response to Skardon’s death has been immense and heartfelt. Tributes have flowed from national military leaders, the White Sands Missile Range community, Clemson University officials, and countless individuals who were touched by his story. Social media was flooded with messages of gratitude and respect, with many sharing personal anecdotes of meeting him at the march or hearing him speak. Varying organizations, including the Bataan Memorial Death March committee, issued statements celebrating his life and lamenting his loss. His contributions to fostering understanding of the POW experience and ensuring the sacrifices of his comrades were not forgotten were consistently highlighted. This collective grief underscores the deep reverence held for Skardon and others like him. Colonel Skardon's passing has a significant impact on the veteran community and organizations dedicated to historical preservation. His role as a living link to the Bataan Death March and the broader WWII POW experience was invaluable. His advocacy for remembrance, particularly through his participation in the annual march, raised significant awareness and funding for veteran support initiatives and memorials. His legacy will continue to influence how military history is taught and how veterans are honored. Organizations like the American Legion, Veterans of Foreign Wars, and various POW/MIA groups often drew inspiration from his life, using his story to highlight the importance of their missions. The Bataan Memorial Death March itself will undoubtedly continue in his spirit, with future events likely dedicating themselves to his enduring memory, impacting the event's structure and promotional efforts. The future outlook for the remembrance of WWII veterans and the Bataan Death March is shaped by Skardon's powerful example. While the number of living WWII veterans continues to dwindle, figures like Skardon ensure their stories and sacrifices are indelibly etched into national consciousness. His life underscores the ongoing need for educational programs and initiatives that preserve these critical historical narratives. His legacy will serve as a continuous reminder of the human cost of war and the importance of peace and international understanding. The torch of remembrance, once carried by Skardon, is now firmly passed to younger generations, tasked with upholding the values he exemplified and ensuring that the lessons of history are never forgotten. Colonel Ben Hagood Skardon's life was a testament to courage, resilience, and unwavering dedication. From the hellish conditions of a Japanese POW camp to the revered halls of academia and the inspiring paths of remembrance marches, he lived a life of profound impact. His death at 101 marks the end of an era, but his extraordinary legacy of fortitude, forgiveness, and patriotism will undoubtedly continue to inspire for generations to come.
