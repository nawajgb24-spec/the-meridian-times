Europe and World Unite Against Death Penalty: Call for Global Abolition Echoes

On the European and World Day against the Death Penalty, the Council of Europe and the European Union issued a powerful joint statement, reiterating their unwavering commitment to the global abolition of capital punishment. This annual declaration by Marija Pejčinović Burić and Josep Borrell underscores the shared belief that the death penalty is a cruel, inhuman, and degrading punishment that violates fundamental human rights, including the right to life. The statement highlights Europe's near-complete abolition of capital punishment as a model and calls for a universal moratorium on executions, addressing concerns about its arbitrary application and irreversible nature. The article delves into the historical context, legal frameworks, and ongoing diplomatic efforts to achieve a world free from capital punishment, examining the challenges and future prospects of this critical human rights campaign.

## Europe and World Unite Against Death Penalty: Call for Global Abolition Echoes **STRASBOURG/BRUSSELS** – On October 10th, the occasion of the European and World Day against the Death Penalty, the Council of Europe (CoE) and the European Union (EU) issued a joint statement, reaffirming their steadfast opposition to capital punishment. The declaration, signed by Marija Pejčinović Burić, Secretary General of the Council of Europe, and Josep Borrell, High Representative of the European Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy, underscored the fundamental belief that the death penalty is a cruel, inhuman, and degrading punishment, incompatible with human dignity. The annual statement serves as a powerful reminder of Europe’s unique status as a continent nearly free of capital punishment and reinforces the collective call for its universal abolition. Both institutions reiterated their commitment to advocating for the end of executions globally, emphasizing that capital punishment has no place in modern, democratic societies. ### Background of Abolition The commitment to abolish the death penalty is deeply embedded in the legal and ethical frameworks of both the Council of Europe and the European Union. For decades, Europe has been a leading force in the global movement against capital punishment, driven by the belief in the inherent right to life and the prohibition of torture and inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment. The Council of Europe has been instrumental in establishing a continent-wide ban on the death penalty. Its human rights framework, particularly the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), provides the bedrock for this stance. The EU, in turn, has made abolition of the death penalty a key element of its external policy, advocating for it in its relations with third countries and through its multilateral diplomacy. This principled opposition is rooted in several core arguments. The death penalty is irreversible; any miscarriage of justice, which is inherent in any legal system, cannot be undone. It disproportionately affects individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds and often those belonging to racial, ethnic, or religious minorities. Furthermore, extensive research has consistently failed to provide credible evidence that capital punishment deters crime more effectively than life imprisonment. ### A Decades-Long Timeline of Progress The journey towards a death penalty-free Europe and a globally abolitionist stance has been marked by significant milestones: **1950:** The European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) is adopted. While not explicitly abolishing the death penalty, it lays the groundwork for later protocols by enshrining the right to life and prohibiting inhuman or degrading treatment. **1983:** Protocol No. 6 to the ECHR is adopted, abolishing the death penalty in peacetime. This was a critical step, making it binding for signatory states to ensure no one within their jurisdiction is executed for crimes committed outside of wartime. **1989:** The United Nations General Assembly adopts the Second Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), aiming at the abolition of the death penalty worldwide. This global instrument complemented regional efforts. **2002:** Protocol No. 13 to the ECHR is adopted, abolishing the death penalty in all circumstances, including for acts committed in time of war or imminent threat of war. This cemented Europe's position as a de jure death penalty-free zone. **2007:** The European Day against the Death Penalty is established, observed annually on October 10th. This date was later broadened to the European and World Day against the Death Penalty, emphasizing the global nature of the campaign. **Annually:** Since 2007, the Council of Europe and the European Union have issued joint statements on October 10th, reiterating their commitment and assessing global progress and setbacks. ### Latest Developments: A Renewed Call for Action The most recent joint statement by Ms. Pejčinović Burić and Mr. Borrell highlighted both progress and areas of concern in the global fight against the death penalty. They welcomed the continuing trend towards abolition, noting that more than two-thirds of countries worldwide have now abolished capital punishment in law or in practice. This reflects a growing international consensus on the inherent barbarity of the punishment. However, the statement also expressed deep concern over instances where the death penalty continues to be applied, particularly in retentionist states. It condemned the use of capital punishment for offenses that do not meet the threshold of ‘most serious crimes’ under international law, such as drug-related offenses, and its application against individuals involved in protests or for political reasons. The officials specifically called for an immediate moratorium on executions in all countries that still retain the death penalty, urging them to move towards full abolition. The joint declaration also stressed the importance of fair trial guarantees and access to legal counsel, particularly in capital cases, where the risk of irreversible error is paramount. It reaffirmed the EU and CoE’s commitment to engaging with countries that retain the death penalty through diplomatic demarches, awareness-raising campaigns, and support for civil society organizations working on the ground. ### Analysis: A Consistent Stance Amidst Global Challenges The enduring joint commitment of the Council of Europe and the European Union on this issue is a testament to the centrality of human rights in their foreign policy. Their consistent advocacy provides moral and diplomatic pressure on retentionist states, reinforcing the international legal norms against capital punishment. This unified European voice is crucial in a global landscape where progress towards abolition is not always linear. While Europe has largely achieved abolition, the global context presents ongoing challenges. Some states continue to use capital punishment as a tool of political repression or as a response to perceived threats to national security. The statement's emphasis on fair trial standards and the prohibition of using the death penalty for non-most serious crimes directly addresses these abuses, aligning with established international human rights law. The effectiveness of this advocacy lies in its sustained nature and its comprehensive approach, combining legal instruments, diplomatic engagement, and support for civil society. It demonstrates that the campaign against the death penalty is not merely an ideological stance but a deeply considered policy based on principles of justice, human dignity, and the fallibility of judicial systems. ### Public Response and Advocacy The annual joint statement receives widespread support from international human rights organizations and civil society groups. Organizations such as Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch consistently echo the sentiments of the EU and CoE, providing detailed reports on the global application of the death penalty and highlighting individual cases of concern. These NGOs often use the European and World Day against the Death Penalty to launch their own campaigns, amplify calls for moratoria, and raise public awareness. Public opinion within Europe overwhelmingly supports the abolition of the death penalty, a reflection of the decades of advocacy and the strong legal frameworks in place. Outside Europe, public sentiment varies significantly, with some countries showing strong support for capital punishment. The joint statement's outreach aims to influence these perspectives through education and highlighting the practical arguments against its use. ### Industry Impact: Reshaping Legal and Diplomatic Norms While 'industry impact' typically refers to commercial sectors, in the context of human rights, it pertains to the significant influence on the international legal and diplomatic 'industry' and the operational landscape of human rights advocacy. The consistent stance of the CoE and EU has profoundly shaped international legal discourse, cementing the abolition of the death penalty as a cornerstone of human rights law. This includes influencing the terms of engagement in bilateral and multilateral relations, where respect for human rights, including abolition, can be a condition for cooperation or aid. For legal professionals and human rights advocates globally, the European experience and its robust legal frameworks serve as benchmarks and tools for argumentation in their own national contexts. The sustained pressure from these bodies also impacts the strategies and funding priorities of international NGOs, directing resources towards campaigns in retentionist states and supporting legal aid initiatives. ### Future Outlook: Persistent Engagement for Universal Human Rights The future outlook for global abolition, while promising in terms of the long-term trend, requires persistent engagement. The Council of Europe and the European Union are expected to continue their strong advocacy, using all available diplomatic and legal tools. This includes bilateral dialogues, interventions in international forums like the United Nations, and targeted support for national abolitionist movements. Challenges remain, particularly in regions where the death penalty is deeply entrenched, or where its use has seen a recent resurgence. Belarus, the only country in Europe still carrying out executions, remains a specific focus of concern. The EU and CoE will continue to press for a global moratorium on executions as an immediate, practical step towards the ultimate goal of universal abolition. This incremental approach aims to save lives while working towards fundamental legal and societal change. ### Conclusion: A Vision for a World Without Capital Punishment The European and World Day against the Death Penalty underscores a shared, unwavering vision: a world where no state takes a human life as punishment. The joint statement from the Council of Europe and the European Union serves as a poignant reminder of the moral and legal imperative to abolish capital punishment everywhere. Their consistent advocacy, rooted in decades of legal and diplomatic work, continues to drive the global movement towards a more humane and just international order, upholding the fundamental right to life and dignity for all.
