New World Screwworm Detected in Doña Ana, Hidalgo Counties

Cases of the New World screwworm, a highly destructive livestock parasite, have been confirmed in Doña Ana and Hidalgo counties, New Mexico. This re-emergence marks a significant challenge for agricultural and public health officials, as the pest was eradicated from the continental U.S. in 1966. Federal and state agencies are implementing strict quarantine measures, enhanced surveillance, and a sterile insect release program to contain and eradicate the threat, emphasizing the importance of public awareness and cooperation.

A parasitic threat long considered eradicated from the continental United States has re-emerged in New Mexico, with confirmed cases of New World screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax) reported in Doña Ana and Hidalgo counties. This development has prompted a coordinated, urgent response from federal and state agricultural agencies to contain and eliminate the pest. New World screwworms are fly larvae that infest the open wounds of warm-blooded animals, including livestock, wildlife, and even humans. If left untreated, these infestations can be debilitating or even fatal, posing a significant risk to the region's robust agricultural sector and native animal populations. The United States successfully eradicated the New World screwworm in 1966, a landmark achievement in veterinary public health. This success was largely due to the implementation of the Sterile Insect Technique (SIT), where millions of sterile male flies are released to mate with wild females, thereby preventing offspring. A perpetual SIT program in Panama has historically served as a biological barrier, preventing the screwworm's re-entry into North America. The re-discovery of the screwworm in New Mexico represents a serious biosecurity challenge. It underscores the constant vigilance required to protect livestock and wildlife from foreign animal diseases and invasive pests, even those long considered vanquished. The initial confirmation in New Mexico came in October 2023, when a domestic goat in Doña Ana County was identified with screwworm larvae. The samples were sent to the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s (USDA) National Veterinary Services Laboratories (NVSL) for definitive diagnosis, which subsequently confirmed the presence of Cochliomyia hominivorax. Following the initial detection, intensive surveillance and testing efforts were immediately launched. These efforts led to the identification of additional cases in domestic livestock within Hidalgo County in late 2023 and early 2024, indicating a localized, albeit contained, spread of the parasite. Upon confirmation of the cases, the USDA's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS), in collaboration with the New Mexico Department of Agriculture (NMDA) and the New Mexico Livestock Board (NMLB), activated emergency response protocols. These protocols are designed for rapid containment and eradication of high-consequence agricultural pests. Key to the immediate response was the establishment of quarantine zones in Doña Ana and Hidalgo counties. These zones restrict the movement of susceptible animals to prevent further dissemination of the screwworm. This measure is critical for isolating the affected areas and concentrating eradication efforts. Within the quarantine areas, mandatory inspections are required for all warm-blooded animals, including livestock and domestic pets, before they can be moved out of the restricted zone. These inspections aim to detect any potential infestations and ensure that only screwworm-free animals are transported. Analysis of the re-emergence focuses on understanding the pathway of introduction. While the exact source of the current New Mexico outbreak remains unconfirmed, such re-introductions can occur through various means, including the movement of infested animals, international travel, or climatic factors altering established pest boundaries. Officials are assessing the genetic profiles of the detected screwworms to determine if they are related to known strains from other regions or if they represent a novel incursion. This genetic analysis helps inform the most effective eradication strategies and strengthens future biosecurity measures. The public response to the outbreak has been largely cooperative, driven by awareness campaigns launched by state agencies. Residents in affected and surrounding counties are urged to remain vigilant for signs of screwworm infestations in their animals and to report any suspected cases to authorities immediately. Pet owners, particularly those with animals that have open wounds or have recently undergone surgery, are advised to take extra precautions. They are encouraged to inspect their pets regularly and contact their veterinarian if they observe any suspicious activity, such as maggots in a wound. For the agricultural industry, the re-emergence of the New World screwworm poses a direct and substantial threat. Livestock producers face potential economic losses due to animal illness, reduced productivity, and the costs associated with increased surveillance, treatment, and compliance with movement restrictions. The dairy, beef, and sheep industries in New Mexico are particularly vulnerable. The presence of screwworm can disrupt breeding programs, impact animal welfare, and potentially lead to trade restrictions if not swiftly controlled. Eradication programs, while necessary, also impose significant financial burdens on government agencies and indirectly on producers. Looking ahead, the eradication strategy hinges on the continued deployment of the Sterile Insect Technique. Sterile male screwworm flies are being released in the affected areas, inundating the wild population and drastically reducing the chances of successful reproduction. This method has proven highly effective in previous eradication efforts. Intensive surveillance will continue for an extended period after the last confirmed case to ensure complete eradication. This includes regular inspections of livestock, monitoring wildlife populations, and maintaining public awareness campaigns. The goal is to declare the region screwworm-free once rigorous criteria are met. Ongoing collaborative efforts between federal, state, and local agencies, alongside proactive public participation, will be crucial in mitigating the risk of further spread and achieving successful eradication. The incident serves as a stark reminder of the persistent biosecurity threats facing agricultural communities and the importance of robust response mechanisms. The re-discovery of New World screwworm in Doña Ana and Hidalgo counties represents a serious challenge to New Mexico’s agricultural health. Through a rapid and coordinated response, including quarantine measures, enhanced surveillance, and the proven Sterile Insect Technique, authorities are working to eliminate the threat. The success of these efforts will depend on sustained agency collaboration and vigilant public and industry cooperation, aiming to restore the region’s screwworm-free status and protect its vital animal resources.
