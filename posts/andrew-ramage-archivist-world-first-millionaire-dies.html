Andrew Ramage, Archivist Who Uncovered 'World's First Millionaire,' Dies at 69

Andrew Ramage, the meticulous archivist whose discovery of 17th-century financial records revealed George Audley as potentially the world's first millionaire, has died at 69. His groundbreaking work at the Westminster City Archives transformed understandings of early modern wealth and capitalism, leaving an indelible mark on economic history.

London – Andrew Ramage, the acclaimed archivist and historian whose meticulous research brought to light the financial dealings of George Audley, often credited as the ‘world’s first millionaire,’ has died. He passed away on January 16, 2024, at the age of 69. Ramage’s pivotal discovery in 2005 at the Westminster City Archives fundamentally reshaped historical understanding of wealth accumulation in the early modern period. His work provided concrete evidence of unprecedented individual fortune, previously thought to be unimaginable outside royal circles at that time. Born into an era of burgeoning historical interest, Andrew Ramage dedicated his career to the painstaking process of uncovering the past through forgotten documents. He distinguished himself through a diligent approach to archival research, where hidden truths often lie buried for centuries within dusty ledgers and brittle manuscripts. His role as an archivist at the Westminster City Archives placed him at the heart of London’s rich historical record. It was this access, coupled with his keen eye and profound historical curiosity, that enabled his most significant contribution to scholarship. The early 17th century marked a transformative period in England, characterized by growing trade, evolving financial instruments, and the nascent stages of global commerce. Understanding the personal fortunes amassed during this era offers crucial insights into the broader economic landscape. George Audley, the subject of Ramage’s celebrated findings, was a formidable London moneylender and property speculator during the Stuart period. His financial acumen allowed him to navigate the complex economic currents of his time, amassing a fortune that astonished contemporaries and historians alike. Ramage’s breakthrough came when he unearthed Audley's extensive financial receipts. These documents detailed a vast network of loans, investments, and property holdings, painting a comprehensive picture of a financial empire that stretched across London and beyond. The receipts provided a granular view of Audley’s wealth, estimated at £400,000 at the time of his death in 1668. News of Andrew Ramage’s passing has elicited tributes from across the historical and archival communities. His contributions are being recognized as fundamental to understanding the origins of modern financial systems. The revelation of Audley’s vast fortune, meticulously documented by Ramage, offered a compelling counter-narrative to existing assumptions about historical wealth distribution. It demonstrated that significant private capital accumulation was occurring earlier and on a greater scale than many historians had previously accepted. Ramage’s work underscored the vital role of primary source research in historical scholarship. By bringing overlooked archival materials to prominence, he demonstrated how detailed investigation can profoundly alter established narratives and open new avenues for academic inquiry. His findings did not merely identify a wealthy individual; they illuminated the sophisticated financial mechanisms, legal structures, and societal values that enabled such wealth creation in 17th-century England. This included practices like usury, land speculation, and intricate lending arrangements, all meticulously detailed in the unearthed receipts. The public response to Ramage's discovery was largely one of fascination and scholarly approval. Economic historians lauded the depth of his research, while the broader public gained a tangible connection to the origins of immense personal wealth. Ramage’s contribution spurred further investigation into the financial lives of other prominent figures of the era, leading to a richer, more nuanced understanding of economic power dynamics. It encouraged a renewed focus on the practicalities of wealth management in historical contexts, moving beyond theoretical models to empirical evidence.
