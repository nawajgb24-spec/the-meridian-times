### Confirmed New World Screwworm Detections Prompt Federal Response **WASHINGTON D.C.** — The U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) has confirmed detections of the New World Screwworm (NWSM), a highly destructive parasitic fly, in U.S. territory. These confirmed cases have initiated a rapid and comprehensive response from federal and state agencies, underscoring the serious threat this insect poses to the nation's livestock, pets, and wildlife populations. The NWSM, scientifically known as *Cochliomyia hominivorax*, is notorious for its larvae, which infest the open wounds of warm-blooded animals. These larvae feed on living tissue, causing severe damage, secondary infections, and potentially death if left untreated. The re-emergence of this pest, which was eradicated from the United States decades ago, necessitates an immediate and coordinated effort to prevent its establishment and wider spread. ### Background: A History of Eradication The New World Screwworm historically plagued the southern United States, causing billions of dollars in losses to the agricultural industry. Its life cycle involves adult flies laying eggs in the wounds of animals. The hatching larvae then burrow into the flesh, feeding and growing, leading to painful and often fatal infestations. The United States successfully eradicated the NWSM in 1966 through a monumental effort led by the USDA. This historic achievement relied heavily on the Sterile Insect Technique (SIT). SIT involves rearing vast numbers of screwworms, sterilizing male flies with radiation, and then releasing them into infested areas. When sterile males mate with wild females, no offspring are produced, thereby reducing the wild population over time. The eradication program was so successful that it was expanded to Mexico and Central America, establishing a permanent barrier zone in the Darien Gap on the border between Panama and Colombia. This barrier, maintained by a cooperative international program, is designed to prevent screwworms from re-entering areas to the north. ### Timeline of Recent Detections The most significant recent re-emergence of the New World Screwworm occurred in September 2016. APHIS confirmed the presence of NWSM larvae in Key deer in the Florida Keys, Monroe County, Florida. This discovery marked the first time the insect had been detected in the U.S. in over 30 years outside of port-of-entry interceptions. Following the initial detections, the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services (FDACS) declared an agricultural state of emergency for Monroe County in early October 2016. Federal and state quarantines were swiftly put in place to restrict the movement of animals from the affected zone. By mid-October 2016, APHIS began releasing sterile screwworm flies in the Florida Keys. This immediate re-deployment of the proven SIT strategy was crucial to disrupting the reproductive cycle of the wild screwworm population. Intensive surveillance and animal inspections were also ramped up across the region. Through sustained efforts, including ongoing sterile fly releases and public awareness campaigns, the Florida Keys were declared free of the New World Screwworm in March 2017, demonstrating the effectiveness of a coordinated rapid response. ### Latest Developments: Vigilance Continues While the 2016-2017 Florida outbreak was successfully contained, the threat of NWSM re-introduction remains constant, particularly in southern border states. APHIS maintains a robust surveillance program and collaborates with state animal health officials, veterinarians, and livestock producers to monitor for any signs of the pest. Recent confirmed detections have triggered localized responses, including the establishment of Animal Health Emergency Zones and the implementation of movement restrictions for animals. These zones typically require veterinary inspections and certifications for animals leaving the affected areas. APHIS is working closely with state partners, such as the Texas Animal Health Commission (TAHC) in areas of potential risk, to conduct targeted surveillance and response activities. These include trapping adult flies, inspecting livestock and pets for wounds, and analyzing suspect larvae samples at diagnostic laboratories. ### Analysis: The Threat and Response Strategy The New World Screwworm poses a multifaceted threat. Its parasitic nature directly impacts the health and welfare of a wide range of warm-blooded animals. Cattle, sheep, goats, swine, and horses are particularly vulnerable, but pets like dogs and cats, and even wildlife such as deer, bears, and birds, can become infested. From an ecological perspective, an established NWSM population could decimate wildlife populations, especially endangered species with limited numbers. For instance, the Key deer population, which faced the 2016 infestation, is a federally endangered species. The re-eradication strategy hinges on the rapid and targeted deployment of the Sterile Insect Technique. APHIS maintains a screwworm rearing facility in Panama that produces millions of sterile flies weekly. This facility is critical for supplying sterile insects quickly to any re-emergent outbreak in the U.S. or Mexico. Beyond SIT, effective containment relies on robust diagnostic capabilities, strict animal movement controls, and public education. The ability to identify NWSM quickly and prevent infected animals from moving to new areas is paramount. ### Public Response: Community Vigilance and Biosecurity Public awareness and cooperation are vital components of any NWSM eradication effort. APHIS and state agricultural departments issue advisories to livestock producers, pet owners, and veterinarians, detailing how to identify screwworm infestations and what actions to take. Livestock owners are urged to regularly inspect their animals for wounds and report any suspicious larvae to their veterinarian or state animal health officials immediately. Pet owners are advised to keep their animals' wounds clean and covered, and to seek veterinary care for any persistent or unexplained wound issues. Biosecurity measures, such as careful inspection of animals being moved into or out of affected areas, are emphasized. The goal is to minimize the chances of the screwworm hitchhiking on animals to new, uninfested locations. Posters, brochures, and online resources are widely disseminated to inform the public. ### Industry Impact: Economic Consequences and Preparedness An established NWSM population would have severe economic consequences for the U.S. agricultural industry. Livestock producers would face increased veterinary costs for treating infested animals, potential losses due to animal deaths, and decreased productivity from affected herds. The beef, dairy, swine, and small ruminant industries could experience significant disruptions, impacting trade and market access. Export markets, particularly for live animals and animal products, could be jeopardized if the U.S. were no longer considered NWSM-free. Beyond direct costs, the need for ongoing surveillance and control measures would represent a considerable financial burden on state and federal budgets. The industry's ability to quickly implement biosecurity protocols and report suspected cases plays a crucial role in mitigating these impacts and safeguarding the nation's animal agriculture sector. ### Future Outlook: Sustained Effort for Eradication The immediate future involves continued intensive surveillance and the targeted application of control measures in any newly detected areas. APHIS's long-term strategy remains focused on maintaining the NWSM-free status of the U.S. and supporting the integrity of the barrier zone in Panama. Research continues into enhancing diagnostic tools and improving the efficiency of the Sterile Insect Technique, although SIT remains the cornerstone of eradication. Investment in emergency preparedness and response capabilities is ongoing, ensuring that resources can be mobilized swiftly to address any future incursions. Collaboration with Mexico and Central American countries is also critical. A strong international partnership helps to reinforce the barrier zone and prevent the northern migration of the pest. The collective effort across borders is essential for regional security against NWSM. ### Conclusion: Vigilance as a National Priority Confirmed detections of the New World Screwworm serve as a reminder of the persistent threat posed by exotic pests and diseases. While eradication efforts have historically been successful, the continuous vigilance of federal and state agencies, coupled with the active participation of livestock producers, pet owners, and the general public, remains paramount. Maintaining the NWSM-free status of the United States is a national priority, safeguarding animal health, protecting biodiversity, and ensuring the economic stability of the agricultural sector. The ongoing, coordinated response demonstrates a commitment to preventing this destructive parasite from re-establishing itself within U.S. borders.
