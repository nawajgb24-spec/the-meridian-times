Parachute Science Undermines Tropical Insect Conservation Efforts

The pervasive practice of 'parachute science' is increasingly identified as a critical impediment to understanding and conserving tropical insect populations. Researchers from high-income nations often conduct studies in biodiverse regions, collecting data and samples with limited meaningful collaboration or benefit to local scientists and communities. This approach stifles local scientific development, skews research priorities, and creates significant gaps in long-term ecological data crucial for addressing insect declines. Calls for more equitable, collaborative, and decolonized scientific partnerships are growing, aiming to empower local expertise and foster sustainable conservation strategies.

## Parachute Science Undermines Tropical Insect Conservation Efforts **Introduction** The vibrant, complex ecosystems of tropical regions are home to an estimated 80% of the world's insect species, making them critical hotspots for global biodiversity. However, the scientific study and conservation of these invaluable invertebrates are being significantly hampered by a long-standing practice known as 'parachute science.' This model, where researchers from high-income countries undertake fieldwork in biodiverse, low-income nations without robust local collaboration, is increasingly recognized as a profound barrier to effective ecological understanding and conservation. **Background** Parachute science, sometimes referred to as 'helicopter research' or scientific colonialism, describes a scenario where external scientists conduct expeditions, collect data and samples, and then return to their home institutions to analyze findings and publish papers. Often, local scientists, communities, and institutions in the host countries receive minimal credit, resources, or sustained benefits from these endeavors. This pattern emerged and solidified during colonial eras, continuing into post-colonial periods, driven by disparities in funding, infrastructure, and access to international scientific networks. The implications for tropical insect research are particularly acute. Insects are often small, numerous, and require specialized knowledge for identification and study. A lack of sustained local presence, training, and infrastructure means that much of the long-term monitoring essential for detecting population trends and understanding ecological shifts is either absent or fragmented. This creates substantial gaps in critical data needed to respond to the global insect decline crisis. **Timeline** The critique of extractivist scientific practices began to gain traction in academic circles in the mid-to-late 20th century. Concerns initially focused on issues like bioprospecting and the appropriation of traditional knowledge, particularly after the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) in 1992 emphasized national sovereignty over genetic resources and the fair sharing of benefits. By the early 2000s, the terms 'parachute science' and 'scientific colonialism' became more widely used to describe the broader ethical and practical failures of inequitable research partnerships. The 2010s saw increasing calls from within the scientific community for greater transparency, co-authorship for local researchers, and meaningful capacity building. In the 2020s, with a heightened global awareness of biodiversity loss and social justice, these discussions have intensified, leading to tangible efforts to reform international scientific collaboration. **Latest Developments** Recent years have seen a growing consensus within global scientific communities regarding the need to dismantle parachute science. Major scientific journals and publishing houses are increasingly implementing stricter ethical guidelines, often requiring evidence of equitable partnerships, prior informed consent from local authorities, and fair attribution, including co-authorship for local collaborators. Funding agencies are also beginning to prioritize projects that demonstrate genuine capacity building and long-term engagement with host country institutions. Conservation organizations and entomological societies are actively advocating for new models of research that emphasize co-creation of projects, mutual respect, and the empowerment of scientists from the Global South. These initiatives aim to shift the paradigm from an extractive model to one of true partnership and shared ownership of scientific endeavors and their outcomes. **Analysis** The detrimental impact of parachute science on tropical insect research extends beyond mere ethics to fundamental scientific efficacy. When research priorities are set externally, they may not align with the most pressing local conservation challenges or indigenous knowledge systems. This can lead to fragmented data collection, a focus on easily accessible or charismatic species over less-studied but ecologically vital groups, and an overall lack of continuity in monitoring. Furthermore, the absence of robust local capacity building means that once external researchers leave, the knowledge, skills, and equipment often depart with them. This cycle prevents the development of sustainable, locally-led research programs that are essential for long-term ecological surveillance. For insects, whose populations can fluctuate rapidly and respond acutely to environmental changes, continuous, locally-driven monitoring is indispensable for understanding their status and implementing timely conservation interventions. **Public Response** The scientific community's response to the critique of parachute science has been largely positive, though implementation challenges remain. Academics and researchers, particularly younger generations and those from underrepresented regions, are increasingly vocal about the need for systemic change. Calls for 'decolonizing science' resonate across various disciplines, advocating for a fundamental re-evaluation of power dynamics in global research. Many institutions now recognize the ethical imperative and scientific benefits of equitable collaboration. This shift is not merely about fairness; it is about improving the quality and relevance of research by incorporating diverse perspectives and local expertise. There is a growing understanding that local scientists often possess invaluable ecological knowledge, language skills, and established community relationships that are crucial for effective fieldwork and data interpretation. **Industry Impact** The 'industry' of scientific research – encompassing academia, funding bodies, publishers, and international organizations – is slowly adapting. Major scientific associations are developing codes of conduct for international collaboration. For instance, the International Union of Biological Sciences (IUBS) and others have emphasized the importance of intellectual property rights, benefit-sharing, and capacity building in their guidelines. Publishers are increasingly scrutinizing submissions to ensure ethical research practices have been followed, particularly regarding collaborations with researchers in the Global South. This impacts how projects are designed, funded, and executed, pushing towards more genuinely collaborative models that foster mutual respect and long-term relationships rather than short-term extractive expeditions. **Future Outlook** The future of tropical insect research hinges on a concerted shift away from parachute science towards truly equitable and collaborative models. This involves significant investment in local scientific infrastructure, training programs, and mentorship initiatives. Empowering scientists in tropical countries to lead research, set agendas, and access international funding streams will be paramount. Sustainable solutions will also require fostering interdisciplinary approaches that integrate traditional ecological knowledge with modern scientific methods. The goal is to build long-term partnerships that ensure not only the ethical conduct of science but also the production of more comprehensive, relevant, and actionable research outcomes for the preservation of tropical insect biodiversity. **Conclusion** 'Parachute science' presents a significant hurdle to effective tropical insect conservation. By perpetuating imbalances in resources, recognition, and research priorities, it undermines the very efforts needed to understand and protect Earth's most diverse yet vulnerable insect populations. Moving forward, the global scientific community must embrace genuine collaboration, capacity building, and the decolonization of research to ensure that vital knowledge is co-created, shared equitably, and ultimately used to safeguard these essential components of our planet's ecosystems.
