A Call for Realism: Reshaping 'Gardeners' World' for Modern Britain

Gardener and author Claire Ratinon has sparked a nationwide conversation with her recent call for BBC's 'Gardeners' World' to broaden its portrayal of British gardens, moving beyond idealized, manicured spaces to embrace more 'messy, realistic chaos.' Her critique highlights a growing desire among the public for gardening media to reflect diverse practices, sustainability, and the everyday challenges faced by gardeners, prompting a deeper look into the cultural role and future direction of Britain's most beloved gardening program.

### Introduction A recent critique by prominent gardener and author Claire Ratinon has ignited a significant discussion regarding the portrayal of British gardens on mainstream television, particularly on the BBC's long-running and highly influential 'Gardeners' World'. Ratinon's argument, articulated in The Guardian, suggests that the programme should move beyond predominantly showcasing idealized, often immaculately maintained spaces, to embrace the 'messy, realistic chaos' that characterizes many genuine British gardens. This perspective resonates with a growing segment of the gardening public who advocate for greater authenticity, sustainability, and inclusivity in horticultural media. The debate transcends mere aesthetic preferences, touching upon deeper questions of representation, environmental responsibility, and the accessibility of gardening for everyone, regardless of garden size, skill level, or budget. ### Background 'Gardeners' World' first aired in 1968, quickly establishing itself as a cornerstone of British television. Over its decades-long run, it has become synonymous with practical gardening advice, inspiring visuals, and a calming, informative tone. Past presenters like Percy Thrower and Alan Titchmarsh, and current host Monty Don, have cultivated a loyal viewership, often inviting audiences into their own personal gardens, most notably Don's Longmeadow. The programme’s traditional aesthetic has often leaned towards showcasing gardens that are well-tended, abundant, and visually pristine. While undeniably beautiful and aspirational, this approach has, for some, created a disconnect with the realities of everyday gardening. Many British gardens, whether due to time constraints, financial limitations, or a conscious choice towards more naturalistic methods, do not conform to this polished ideal. As gardening culture has evolved, so too have public expectations. There is an increasing awareness of the ecological benefits of less formal gardening, including supporting biodiversity, mitigating climate change, and reducing resource consumption. These evolving priorities form the backdrop against which Ratinon's call for a more representative 'Gardeners' World' is now being heard. ### Timeline **1968:** 'Gardeners' World' makes its debut on BBC Television, with Percy Thrower as its inaugural lead presenter, setting the tone for instructional and aspirational gardening content. **1996:** Alan Titchmarsh assumes the role of lead presenter, continuing the tradition of practical advice blended with visits to impressive gardens and his own horticultural projects. **2003:** Monty Don takes over as the main presenter. His personal garden, Longmeadow, becomes a central and much-loved feature of the programme, showcasing a slightly more relaxed, yet still extensively cultivated, style. **Early 2000s-Present:** A gradual but significant shift in public gardening interest occurs, moving towards themes of sustainability, rewilding, and ecological gardening, driven by environmental concerns and a desire for more achievable gardening methods. **June 2024:** Claire Ratinon publishes her opinion piece in The Guardian, articulating a widespread sentiment for 'Gardeners' World' to reflect the broader, more chaotic, and realistic spectrum of British gardens, initiating a fresh public discourse. ### Latest Developments Claire Ratinon's article has specifically highlighted a perceived gap between the aspirational gardens frequently featured on 'Gardeners' World' and the lived experience of many British gardeners. She argues that showing more relatable, imperfect spaces would not only be more inclusive but also offer practical inspiration for those navigating real-world gardening challenges like weeds, pests, and limited time. Her critique suggests that by showcasing only the 'best' examples, the programme inadvertently sets unrealistic standards, potentially deterring new gardeners or making existing ones feel inadequate. The emphasis is not on abandoning beauty, but on expanding the definition of what constitutes a beautiful or successful garden to include those that prioritize ecological function and personal connection over aesthetic perfection. While the BBC has not issued a direct, specific response to Ratinon's article, 'Gardeners' World' has, over the years, featured a range of gardens and presenters who champion various approaches, including more wildlife-friendly or productive spaces. However, the core aesthetic often remains rooted in a degree of horticultural control and order. ### Analysis Ratinon's intervention arrives at a pivotal moment for gardening culture in the UK. There is a palpable shift away from purely ornamental, high-maintenance gardens towards spaces that are functional, sustainable, and biodiverse. This includes embracing native plants, creating habitats for wildlife, and allowing certain levels of 'messiness' that are beneficial for ecosystems. For a programme as influential as 'Gardeners' World', reflecting these changing values is crucial for maintaining relevance and broad appeal. The show acts as a guide and an educator for millions, and its visual language shapes public perception of what gardening 'should' look like. By broadening its scope, it could empower more people to garden confidently, without feeling pressured to conform to an unattainable ideal. Furthermore, the discussion touches upon the broader issue of media representation. Just as other sectors of media are scrutinised for their diversity and authenticity, so too is gardening. A more inclusive portrayal of gardens could better represent the diverse demographics of Britain's gardeners and the varied landscapes they cultivate, from urban balconies to sprawling rural plots. ### Public Response The public response to Ratinon's article has been considerable, generating widespread debate across social media, gardening forums, and comment sections. Many gardeners have voiced agreement, sharing their own experiences of 'messy' gardens and expressing a desire to see more relatable examples on screen. Comments often highlight the practical difficulties of achieving the 'Gardeners' World' aesthetic, citing time constraints, physical limitations, and the sheer challenge of maintaining perfection in unpredictable British weather. There is a strong sentiment that seeing more realistic gardens would be encouraging and make gardening feel less intimidating. Conversely, some loyal viewers defend the programme's current format, appreciating its aspirational quality and the escapism it provides. They argue that 'Gardeners' World' serves as an ideal to strive for, offering inspiration and demonstrating best practices. The BBC has a delicate balance to strike between maintaining its beloved traditional format and adapting to contemporary demands for realism and diversity. ### Industry Impact A potential shift in the aesthetic presented by 'Gardeners' World' could have ripple effects throughout the horticultural industry. If the programme were to increasingly champion naturalistic, biodiversity-focused, or less formal gardens, this could influence consumer demand. We might see an increased interest in native plant species, wildflower mixes, and plants that require less intensive care, potentially shifting sales patterns in garden centres and nurseries. There could also be a greater emphasis on products that support wildlife, such as insect hotels and bird feeders, or sustainable gardening tools and practices. Additionally, landscape designers and gardening educators might find themselves increasingly catering to clients and students seeking more ecologically sound and less labour-intensive garden solutions. The narrative promoted by such a prominent show can shape trends, impacting everything from seed sales to garden tourism, by altering public perceptions of what constitutes a desirable garden. ### Future Outlook The ongoing conversation suggests that 'Gardeners' World', and gardening media in general, faces a future where authenticity and ecological consciousness are increasingly valued. While the programme's core appeal – offering advice and inspiration – is likely to remain, its methods of delivery and the types of gardens it highlights may evolve. Future iterations might feature a more deliberate balance between the traditionally aspirational and the realistically achievable, perhaps dedicating segments to gardens that prioritize biodiversity or demonstrate sustainable practices within a 'messy' context. There could be a greater emphasis on problem-solving for common gardening dilemmas, showcasing how to manage a garden rather than just present a perfect finished product. The challenge for the BBC will be to integrate these evolving perspectives without alienating its long-standing audience. A nuanced approach that acknowledges the beauty in imperfection and the value of ecological function, while retaining its signature quality and expertise, could ensure 'Gardeners' World' continues to thrive as a relevant and beloved institution for generations of British gardeners. ### Conclusion Claire Ratinon's compelling argument for a more 'messy, realistic chaos' on 'Gardeners' World' has opened a vital dialogue about the future of gardening representation. It underscores a broader cultural shift towards valuing sustainability, biodiversity, and accessibility in our green spaces. While 'Gardeners' World' has a cherished legacy of inspiring millions, the call for greater realism reflects a desire for media that mirrors the diverse, imperfect, and ecologically rich reality of Britain's gardens. How the programme, and indeed the wider horticultural community, responds to this sentiment will shape not only what we see on our screens but also how we approach and appreciate the gardens that enrich our lives.
