Global Tolls: El Niño's $11 Trillion Shadow, Mozambique and Yemen Crises Unfold

This report examines three critical global developments: the mounting long-term economic cost of El Niño, recently quantified at potentially $11 trillion in lost income from just two major past events; the persistent insurgency and humanitarian crisis in Mozambique's Cabo Delgado province; and the ongoing conflict and severe humanitarian situation in Yemen, exacerbated by recent Red Sea shipping disruptions. It delves into the background, latest developments, and future implications of these disparate yet globally significant challenges, highlighting their far-reaching human and economic consequences.

## Global Tolls: El Niño's $11 Trillion Shadow, Mozambique and Yemen Crises Unfold ### Introduction Across the globe, distinct yet interconnected challenges are shaping geopolitical landscapes and economic stability. From the long-term financial ramifications of El Niño climate patterns, now estimated to run into trillions of dollars, to the enduring conflicts in Mozambique and Yemen, populations are grappling with complex crises. These issues, while diverse in origin and nature, underscore the fragile balance of global stability, environmental resilience, and humanitarian security. ### Background El Niño, a natural climate phenomenon characterized by warming ocean surface temperatures in the central and eastern tropical Pacific Ocean, significantly influences global weather patterns. It can lead to severe droughts in some regions, heavy rainfall and flooding in others, and disrupt agricultural cycles, fisheries, and public health worldwide. Its economic impact, however, has traditionally been difficult to quantify comprehensively until recent scientific endeavors. In Mozambique, the northern province of Cabo Delgado has been engulfed by an insurgency since 2017. Led by a group locally known as Ahlu Sunnah Wa-Jama'ah (ASWJ), or more broadly identified as ISIS-Mozambique, the conflict has created a severe humanitarian crisis. The region is rich in natural gas, and the violence has directly impacted major energy projects, drawing international security intervention. The Republic of Yemen has been in a state of civil war since late 2014, when the Houthi movement (Ansar Allah) seized the capital, Sana'a. This escalated into a broader regional conflict with the intervention of a Saudi-led coalition in 2015. The war has devastated the country's infrastructure, economy, and social fabric, leading to one of the world's most severe humanitarian crises, characterized by widespread food insecurity and displacement. ### Timeline The 1997-98 El Niño event, one of the strongest on record, was later identified as a significant precursor to long-term global economic losses. Similarly, the 2015-16 El Niño, another powerful event, has been linked to substantial future financial tolls. These events set the stage for later research quantifying their far-reaching economic consequences. In Mozambique, the insurgency in Cabo Delgado commenced in October 2017. By March 2021, the violence had intensified significantly, leading TotalEnergies to declare force majeure and suspend its multi-billion-dollar liquefied natural gas (LNG) project in the Afungi peninsula. Later that year, in July 2021, the Southern African Development Community Mission in Mozambique (SAMIM) and Rwandan forces were deployed to assist the Mozambican military in counter-insurgency operations. Yemen's civil war formally intensified in March 2015 with the launch of the Saudi-led coalition's military campaign. Years of conflict and failed peace attempts preceded a significant UN-brokered truce in April 2022. While this truce largely held for six months, it was not formally renewed in October 2022. Despite this, a significant reduction in active fighting continued in many areas. However, late 2023 saw a new escalation with Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping lanes, drawing international military responses in early 2024. ### Latest Developments Recent scientific studies, notably a May 2023 publication in the journal Science by researchers including Justin Mankin of Dartmouth College, have brought new clarity to El Niño's long-term economic impact. This research estimated that the 1997-98 and 2015-16 El Niño events alone cost the global economy approximately $4.1 trillion and $5.7 trillion respectively in lost income over the subsequent five years. Extrapolating these patterns, the cumulative cost of such events could total $84 trillion by the end of the 21st century. In Cabo Delgado, Mozambique, the security situation remains complex. While joint operations by Mozambican, Rwandan, and SAMIM forces have pushed insurgents out of major towns and infrastructure sites, sporadic attacks continue in more remote areas. The departure of some SAMIM contingents has raised concerns about maintaining gains. Humanitarian efforts continue to assist over one million displaced people, with some return programs slowly beginning, though sustainability remains a challenge. TotalEnergies has indicated a potential readiness to resume its LNG project, pending sustained security improvements. For Yemen, diplomatic efforts to secure a lasting peace agreement have faced renewed hurdles. The de-escalation that followed the 2022 truce has been severely tested by the Houthis' attacks on commercial vessels in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden. These actions, undertaken in solidarity with Palestinians in Gaza, have prompted military responses from a U.S.-led international coalition. Meanwhile, the humanitarian crisis persists, with millions facing acute food insecurity and limited access to essential services, despite ongoing aid deliveries. ### Analysis The quantification of El Niño's economic burden fundamentally alters the discourse around climate phenomena. The estimated $11 trillion price tag (a cumulative figure derived from the study's findings for the 1997-98 and 2015-16 events' five-year impacts) underscores that climate-driven disruptions are not merely environmental concerns but profound economic stabilizers, disproportionately affecting developing nations least equipped to adapt. These long-term costs far exceed the immediate damage, impacting growth trajectories for decades. The Mozambican conflict reveals the intertwined nature of security, humanitarian, and economic development challenges. While international military support has yielded tactical successes, the underlying socio-economic grievances that fuel the insurgency require broader, sustained solutions. The fate of major energy projects like TotalEnergies' LNG facility hangs on establishing genuine, long-term stability, which remains elusive in certain areas. Yemen's crisis highlights the entrenched nature of internal and regional proxy conflicts. The Houthis' Red Sea campaign has shifted international attention, linking the Yemeni conflict to broader regional dynamics and global trade routes. This complicates existing peace efforts by adding new layers of stakeholders and interests, potentially making a comprehensive political settlement more distant while deepening the humanitarian plight. ### Public Response The findings on El Niño's economic costs have amplified calls from the scientific community and international organizations for increased investment in climate adaptation and resilience. There's a growing recognition that early warning systems and robust infrastructure can mitigate financial losses, particularly in vulnerable economies. Public discourse is shifting from immediate disaster response to long-term economic planning against climate variability. In Mozambique, humanitarian organizations continue to advocate for increased international aid and access to affected populations. Displaced communities express a desire for lasting peace to allow safe returns and the rebuilding of livelihoods. The government emphasizes its commitment to restoring peace and stability, often highlighting the successes of security operations while acknowledging ongoing challenges. International partners remain engaged, albeit with varying commitments to sustained military presence. Globally, the Red Sea attacks have drawn strong condemnation from maritime industry bodies and governments concerned about shipping safety and economic disruption. Humanitarian organizations continue to stress the dire conditions in Yemen, urging all parties to prioritize civilian protection and facilitate aid access. Public attention is divided between the immediate implications of Red Sea security and the enduring human tragedy on the ground in Yemen. ### Industry Impact El Niño events have far-reaching impacts on key industries. Agriculture and fisheries are directly affected by droughts and floods, leading to crop failures, reduced yields, and disrupted supply chains. The insurance sector faces increased payouts for weather-related damages. Energy demand shifts with extreme temperatures, and global commodity markets can experience volatility due to supply shocks, particularly for agricultural products. This necessitates robust risk management and diversified strategies. In Mozambique, the insurgency has severely hampered the nascent gas industry. TotalEnergies' suspension of its LNG project represents a significant economic setback for the country, delaying projected revenue streams and job creation. Other extractive industries and local businesses in Cabo Delgado have also suffered, leading to job losses and reduced economic activity. Rebuilding investor confidence is paramount for future development. For Yemen, the impact on global shipping through the Red Sea is a critical recent development. Major shipping lines have rerouted vessels around Africa's Cape of Good Hope, increasing transit times, fuel costs, and insurance premiums. This has ripple effects on global supply chains and consumer prices. Domestically, Yemen's economy, already shattered by war, continues to struggle with trade disruptions and lack of investment, further worsening living conditions. ### Future Outlook The scientific community projects that El Niño events may become more frequent or intense due to climate change, suggesting that the long-term economic costs will continue to mount without significant global mitigation and adaptation efforts. Future policies will likely focus on strengthening resilience in agriculture, improving water management, and developing sophisticated early warning systems to cushion economic shocks. Mozambique's future stability hinges on sustained security gains in Cabo Delgado and addressing the root causes of the insurgency, including poverty and lack of opportunity. The potential restart of TotalEnergies' LNG project offers a pathway to economic recovery, but only if local populations see tangible benefits and feel secure. The long-term presence and configuration of international security forces will also be crucial. The path to peace in Yemen remains fraught with challenges. While diplomatic efforts persist, the Red Sea conflict introduces a new layer of complexity, potentially prolonging the stalemate. A comprehensive political settlement involving all parties is essential for alleviating the humanitarian crisis and allowing for national reconstruction. However, as long as regional tensions escalate, the prospects for an immediate breakthrough appear limited. ### Conclusion The world confronts a multifaceted array of challenges, from the profound economic reverberations of natural climate cycles like El Niño to the persistent human cost of conflicts in Mozambique and Yemen. The estimated $11 trillion long-term economic burden associated with past El Niño events underscores the critical need for global climate action and resilience building. Simultaneously, the ongoing crises in Mozambique and Yemen serve as stark reminders of the imperative for sustained diplomatic engagement, humanitarian support, and long-term development strategies. These seemingly disparate issues are united by their profound impact on human lives and global stability. Addressing them effectively requires coordinated international efforts, a commitment to understanding complex causality, and prioritizing sustainable solutions that account for both immediate needs and long-term implications. The global community's ability to navigate these challenges will define the security and prosperity of future generations.
