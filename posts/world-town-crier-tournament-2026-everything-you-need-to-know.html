From royal proclamations and local bylaws to news of births, deaths, and market prices, the crier was the official voice of the community, ensuring widespread dissemination of crucial announcements. Clad in distinctive livery, often a tricorn hat, knee breeches, and a richly embroidered coat, the crier would command attention with a bell and the traditional call, “Oyez, Oyez, Oyez” — an Old French term meaning “hear ye.” Their responsibilities demanded not just a strong voice, but also clear diction, an authoritative presence, and often, an ability to navigate public sentiment. As literacy rates improved and new forms of communication emerged, the formal role of the town crier gradually diminished. However, the mid-20th century saw a significant revival, largely driven by a desire to preserve local heritage and add a unique ceremonial flourish to civic events. Today, town criers are often ambassadors for their towns, participating in festivals, inaugurations, and, notably, competitions. ## Timeline **Medieval Era:** Town criers become established figures across Europe, performing critical communication duties for monarchs, local lords, and municipal governments. **17th-19th Centuries:** The golden age of the town crier, particularly in Britain and its colonies, where they were integral to civic life and news dissemination. **Late 19th / Early 20th Century:** The official, functional role of town criers largely wanes with the rise of mass literacy and modern media like newspapers and radio. **Mid-20th Century:** A cultural resurgence begins, recognizing town crying as a valuable heritage art form.
