Trump's Presidency: Reshaping the Rules-Based World Order

Donald Trump's presidency marked a significant departure from traditional U.S. foreign policy, explicitly challenging the post-World War II rules-based international order. His 'America First' doctrine led to withdrawals from key international agreements, imposition of trade tariffs, and criticisms of long-standing alliances. This article explores the specific actions taken by the Trump administration, their immediate and lingering impacts on global institutions and norms, and the ongoing debate about the resilience and future direction of the international system.

The rules-based international order, a framework of institutions, treaties, and norms largely established after World War II, has underpinned global stability and cooperation for decades. It championed multilateralism, free trade, and collective security. However, the presidency of Donald Trump, from 2017 to 2021, introduced a profound challenge to this established system, prompting a global debate on its resilience and future. Trump's foreign policy, articulated through his 'America First' doctrine, signaled a fundamental shift. It prioritized U.S. national interests and sovereignty above adherence to multilateral frameworks, often questioning the efficacy and fairness of international agreements and institutions. This approach led to a series of actions that many observers characterized as a deliberate effort to recalibrate, if not dismantle, elements of the existing global order. The rules-based order emerged from the ashes of two world wars, spearheaded by the United States and its allies. Its foundational pillars included the United Nations, the Bretton Woods institutions (IMF, World Bank), the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (precursor to the WTO), and collective security alliances like NATO. These structures aimed to prevent future conflicts, promote economic stability, and uphold democratic values through shared responsibilities and international law. Successive U.S. administrations, both Republican and Democratic, largely upheld and strengthened this order, viewing it as essential for American prosperity and security. Engagement with international bodies, promotion of free trade, and maintenance of robust alliances were standard tenets of U.S. foreign policy. This consensus persisted until the arrival of the Trump administration, which viewed aspects of the order as detrimental to American sovereignty and economic standing. The Trump administration's actions unfolded rapidly, signaling its departure from traditional norms. In January 2017, the U.S. formally withdrew from the Trans-Pacific Partnership (TPP), a major trade agreement designed to counter China's influence in Asia. This move was framed as protecting American jobs and industries from perceived unfair competition. Later that year, in June 2017, President Trump announced the U.S. withdrawal from the Paris Agreement on climate change, citing adverse economic impacts on American businesses. The withdrawal officially took effect in November 2020. This decision drew widespread international condemnation, as the agreement represented a global consensus on combating climate change. In May 2018, the U.S. withdrew from the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA), commonly known as the Iran Nuclear Deal, and reimposed sanctions on Tehran. The administration argued the deal was flawed and did not adequately prevent Iran from developing nuclear weapons, despite the consensus of other signatories that Iran was complying. Trade policy became another critical arena for the 'America First' approach. In March 2018, the administration imposed tariffs on steel and aluminum imports, citing national security concerns. These tariffs affected not only adversaries but also traditional allies like Canada, Mexico, and European Union member states, leading to retaliatory tariffs and straining trade relationships. Subsequently, the U.S. initiated a trade war with China, imposing tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars worth of Chinese goods. This move aimed to address perceived unfair trade practices, intellectual property theft, and the large trade deficit, disrupting global supply chains and economic stability. Further challenging the established order, the Trump administration repeatedly criticized NATO allies for insufficient defense spending, suggesting the alliance was obsolete unless members met their financial obligations. While previous administrations had also urged greater burden-sharing, Trump's rhetoric and skepticism regarding Article 5 raised concerns about the U.S. commitment to collective defense. In December 2019, the World Trade Organization's Appellate Body, a critical forum for resolving international trade disputes, effectively ceased to function. This occurred because the U.S. consistently blocked the appointment of new judges, arguing that the body had overstepped its mandate and infringed on national sovereignty. This action crippled a core mechanism of the global trade system. Finally, in July 2020, the U.S. notified the United Nations of its formal withdrawal from the World Health Organization (WHO), effective July 2021. The administration accused the WHO of mishandling the COVID-19 pandemic and being unduly influenced by China. This decision, amid a global health crisis, further underscored a preference for unilateral action over multilateral engagement. The legacy of these actions continues to shape international discourse. While the subsequent Biden administration reversed some withdrawals, such as rejoining the Paris Agreement and the WHO, the precedent set by Trump's policies has generated ongoing uncertainty about U.S. commitment to international norms. Allies, particularly in Europe, began exploring greater strategic autonomy, recognizing a potential shift in U.S. reliability. Analysis of President Trump's impact on the rules-based order reveals a dual perspective. Proponents of 'America First' argued that the U.S. was merely reasserting its sovereign interests and demanding fairer terms from international partners. They contended that some international agreements and institutions were outdated, inefficient, or detrimental to American economic growth and national security, thus requiring renegotiation or withdrawal. Conversely, critics argued that these actions undermined decades of diplomatic effort, weakened crucial international institutions, and diminished U.S. global leadership. They contended that the unilateral approach eroded trust among allies, created vacuums that could be exploited by rival powers, and ultimately made the world less stable and predictable, not more. Public response globally was varied. Among international allies, there was often a sense of bewilderment and concern regarding U.S. policy shifts, leading to increased discussions about European strategic independence and strengthening regional alliances. Some international organizations expressed dismay at the perceived U.S. retreat from global cooperation. Domestically, U.S. public opinion on these foreign policy changes largely mirrored broader political divides. Supporters often lauded the perceived strength and assertiveness of the 'America First' approach, while opponents expressed alarm over the potential long-term damage to U.S. alliances and influence. Experts and academics engaged in extensive debates about whether these changes represented a temporary aberration or a fundamental reorientation of U.S. foreign policy. The industry impact of these policy shifts was significant. Businesses dependent on global supply chains faced increased uncertainty and costs due to tariffs and trade disputes. The agricultural sector, in particular, suffered from retaliatory tariffs imposed by countries like China. The energy sector experienced policy shifts related to fossil fuel production and environmental regulations following the Paris Agreement withdrawal. Investment decisions, cross-border collaborations, and the overall predictability of the global economic landscape were affected. While some domestic industries may have seen short-term benefits from protectionist measures, many multinational corporations and export-oriented businesses reported challenges navigating the new trade environment, highlighting the interconnectedness of the global economy. The future outlook for the rules-based world order remains a subject of intense discussion. While the immediate successor administration sought to re-engage with many international institutions, the experience of the Trump presidency highlighted the fragility of consensus and the potential for nationalist sentiments to challenge established norms. The debate now centers on whether the order can fully recover its previous strength or if it has been irrevocably altered to accommodate a more transactional, nation-first approach. Observers are monitoring whether the U.S. will continue to vacillate between multilateral engagement and unilateral assertion, and how other major powers will adapt to this dynamic. The challenge for the international community is to either reinforce the existing architecture, adapt it to new geopolitical realities, or face a more fragmented and unpredictable global landscape. In conclusion, President Trump's presidency undeniably initiated a period of profound disruption to the rules-based international order. Through his 'America First' doctrine, the U.S. withdrew from key agreements, imposed tariffs, and challenged the efficacy of multilateral institutions and alliances. These actions prompted a significant re-evaluation among global actors regarding the reliability of the U.S. as a consistent anchor of the international system and sparked ongoing debates about the resilience, adaptability, and ultimate future of global governance in a rapidly changing world.
