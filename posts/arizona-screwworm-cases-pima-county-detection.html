Arizona Confirms Seven Screwworm Cases Following Pima County Detection

Arizona state and federal agencies are actively responding to a re-emergence of the New World screwworm, a destructive parasitic fly previously eradicated from the U.S. A total of seven cases have been confirmed since the initial detection in Pima County. The infestation poses a significant threat to the state's livestock, wildlife, and companion animals, prompting the establishment of quarantine zones and a concerted effort to prevent further spread.

Arizona is facing an urgent animal health challenge as authorities confirm a total of seven New World screwworm cases, following an initial detection in Pima County. The parasitic fly, scientifically known as Cochliomyia hominivorax, represents a significant biological threat, having been eradicated from the United States more than half a century ago. Its re-emergence has triggered a robust, coordinated response from state and federal agencies aimed at containment and re-eradication. The New World screwworm is an insect whose larvae infest open wounds of warm-blooded animals. Unlike many other fly larvae that feed on decaying flesh, screwworm larvae consume living tissue, burrowing deeper into the host. This parasitic activity causes immense pain, severe tissue damage, secondary infections, and can ultimately be fatal if left untreated. The economic and ecological ramifications of an uncontrolled outbreak are substantial, affecting agricultural industries, wildlife populations, and companion animals. The United States achieved screwworm eradication in 1966 through an innovative and highly successful program centered on the Sterile Insect Technique (SIT). This method involves raising large numbers of male screwworm flies, sterilizing them with radiation, and then releasing them into infested areas. When sterile males mate with wild females, no offspring are produced, gradually leading to a population decline and eventual eradication. A permanent SIT barrier zone was established along the U.S.-Mexico border to prevent re-infestation from southern regions. The recent timeline of screwworm activity in the U.S. includes a notable outbreak in the Florida Keys in 2016. That incident primarily affected the endangered Key Deer population, leading to an emergency SIT response and ultimately successful re-eradication. The current situation in Arizona marks another critical juncture, demanding similar swift and decisive action to protect the nation's animal health infrastructure. The initial detection in Arizona occurred in late March 2024, when a stray dog in Pima County was confirmed to have a screwworm infestation. This case immediately raised alarms within veterinary and agricultural communities. Following intensive surveillance and diagnostic testing, a total of seven cases have now been confirmed across the affected region, underscoring the urgency of the situation. In response to these latest developments, the Arizona Department of Agriculture (AZDA) and the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) have implemented strict control measures. A quarantine zone has been established, encompassing Pima County and potentially parts of adjacent counties like Maricopa and Pinal, to restrict the movement of animals susceptible to infestation. This measure aims to prevent the parasite from spreading to new areas. Agencies are also conducting active surveillance, including inspections of livestock, pets, and wildlife, to identify and treat any additional cases promptly. The re-emergence of New World screwworm is deeply concerning for several reasons. It poses an immediate and direct threat to animal welfare, potentially causing widespread suffering and mortality. Economically, it could devastate the livestock industry, leading to significant financial losses for ranchers through animal deaths, reduced productivity, and increased veterinary costs. The threat extends to Arizona's diverse wildlife populations, including deer and bighorn sheep, which are vital components of the state's ecosystem. While rare, screwworms can also infest humans, presenting a public health consideration. Public cooperation is identified as a critical component of the eradication strategy. Residents within and near the quarantine zones are urged to inspect their animals regularly for any open wounds or signs of maggot infestation. Suspicious cases should be reported immediately to local veterinarians or the AZDA. Educational campaigns are underway to inform pet owners, livestock producers, and the general public about the signs of screwworm infestation and the importance of adhering to quarantine protocols. This community vigilance is essential for early detection and containment. The agricultural industry, particularly cattle ranching, faces considerable impact. The imposition of quarantine restrictions can disrupt livestock movement for sale, breeding, or grazing, affecting market access and operational efficiency. Ranchers may incur additional expenses for enhanced inspections, preventive treatments, and potential veterinary care for affected animals. The long-term presence of screwworm could lead to trade restrictions on Arizona-origin livestock, impacting both state and national economies. Proactive measures are being emphasized to mitigate these potential economic setbacks. Looking ahead, the immediate focus remains on containing the current infestation and preventing its expansion. This will involve continued aggressive surveillance, rapid diagnosis, and treatment of all confirmed cases. Depending on the scale and persistence of the outbreak, authorities may consider deploying the Sterile Insect Technique on a larger scale, as was done in Florida. The goal is complete re-eradication from Arizona, restoring the state's screwworm-free status and reinforcing the continental barrier against re-infestation. This requires sustained effort and resources from federal and state partners. The confirmation of seven New World screwworm cases in Arizona represents a serious challenge to animal health and economic stability. However, the coordinated response from veterinary professionals, agricultural agencies, and an informed public offers the best path forward for containment and eradication. Vigilance and adherence to established protocols will be paramount in safeguarding Arizona's diverse animal populations and ensuring the long-term success of these critical control efforts.
