This condition results in reduced pigmentation, giving them their characteristic white fur, blue eyes, and dark stripes. This recessive gene trait is extremely rare in the wild. Most white tigers alive today are descendants of a single male tiger named Mohan, captured in India in 1951. Their striking appearance has made them popular attractions in zoos globally, leading to deliberate breeding programs. However, the desire to produce white tigers has often led to inbreeding, as the recessive gene must be inherited from both parents. This practice can result in various health issues, including skeletal deformities such as scoliosis, neurological problems like strabismus (crossed eyes), and organ system complications. Globally, conservation efforts for tigers typically focus on strengthening genetically diverse populations of naturally occurring subspecies in the wild. The ethical considerations surrounding the deliberate breeding of white tigers, given their associated health risks, continue to be a subject of debate among wildlife experts and conservation organizations. ### Timeline: A Broader Historical Context While a specific timeline for the birth of this particular cub is unavailable, the broader history of white tigers in captivity provides context.
