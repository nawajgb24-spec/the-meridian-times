Pennsylvania Ramps Up Defenses Against New World Screwworm

Pennsylvania is taking proactive and stringent measures to prevent the reintroduction of the New World Screwworm, a highly destructive parasitic insect. Eradicated from the United States decades ago, the 'flesh-eating' larvae of the screwworm fly infest warm-blooded animals, including livestock and wildlife, posing a severe threat to the state's agricultural economy and ecological balance. State and federal agencies are collaborating on enhanced surveillance, biosecurity protocols, and public awareness campaigns to safeguard Pennsylvania's animal populations.

Decades of Vigilance: APHIS Sustains Screwworm Eradication Success

The U.S. Department of Agriculture's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) continues its long-standing mission to keep the New World screwworm out of North and Central America. Utilizing the highly effective Sterile Insect Technique, APHIS maintains a crucial biological barrier in Panama, protecting billions of dollars in livestock and wildlife. This persistent effort prevents a devastating parasite from re-establishing itself in regions declared free decades ago, highlighting the ongoing commitment to animal health and agricultural security.

Screwworm Eradication: A Silent Vigil Against a Persistent Threat

The United States Department of Agriculture's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) maintains a continuous vigil against the New World Screwworm, a devastating parasitic fly. Though eradicated from the U.S. decades ago through the innovative Sterile Insect Technique, the threat of reintroduction remains. APHIS actively manages a permanent barrier zone in Panama and conducts rigorous surveillance, as demonstrated by the rapid response to the 2016 Florida Keys outbreak, underscoring the ongoing commitment to protecting American livestock and wildlife from this formidable pest.

New World Screwworm Detections Prompt Vigilance and Rapid Response

Recent confirmed detections of New World Screwworm (NWS) have highlighted the persistent threat this parasitic insect poses to livestock, wildlife, and potentially humans in the United States. While eradicated from the U.S. in 1966, the most significant reintroduction occurred in 2016 in the Florida Keys, triggering an emergency response involving the release of sterile flies, quarantine measures, and intensive surveillance. This event underscored the critical role of agencies like APHIS in rapid containment and eradication, and the ongoing necessity of vigilance to protect animal agriculture and natural ecosystems from this devastating pest.
